Bedtime Stories for 5-Year-Olds: A Free Story and Ideas

Read a free bedtime story for five-year-olds about a garden map, with ideas for gentle adventures and reading together as your child grows.

By Baboo Stories · Updated September 13, 2026

The Map for Tomorrow

Leo spread a piece of paper on the kitchen table.

“I am making a map for tomorrow,” he told Auntie.

He drew the back door, the apple tree and the little patch of mint. Then he drew a large blue circle.

“Is that a pond?” Auntie asked.

“A pond we could build.”

Auntie sat beside him. “Let us put things that are already there on this map. We can make another page for ideas.”

Leo drew a small flowerpot over the blue circle. There was a blue pot by the apple tree, so that fitted nicely.

He added a dotted path from the door to the pot. But the path went straight through the mint.

Leo remembered how carefully they had planted it. He rubbed out a few dots and curved the path around the edge instead.

“Now nobody steps on the leaves,” he said.

At the bottom of the page, Leo drew a square. He could not remember what stood beside the gate.

“We can look in the morning,” said Auntie.

Leo wanted the map to be finished. He held his pencil above the square.

Then he drew a tiny question mark inside it.

“That means we have something to notice tomorrow.”

Auntie found a magnet and helped him put the map on the fridge. Beside it, they pinned a blank page for garden ideas.

Leo washed his hands and chose a book for bedtime.

In bed, he thought of the dotted path curling around the mint. He knew where it began. He knew where it would take him.

And the small square by the gate could wait.

“My map has a resting place,” he said.

“Where?” asked Auntie.

“Here. Until morning.”

Choosing a story for your 5-year-old

Try a story with a clear sequence and a question that can safely wait. Leo changes his map rather than solving every detail at once. If your child enjoys predicting, pause before he draws the question mark. There is no need to ask them to read the words themselves.

Use your child’s interests and response as your guide. Age categories help you browse; they do not mean a child must prefer a certain story length. A familiar picture book is still an option.

Three more story ideas to make your own

These are outlines to tell in your own words, rather than additional complete stories.

  • A child plans a picnic, notices rain and makes an indoor picnic instead. End with the basket unpacked and the plan complete.
  • Two friends draw different routes to the same tree. They compare the paths, choose one and return home before dinner.
  • A little librarian repairs a torn book jacket with help. The book goes back on its shelf, ready for another day.

Make the story shorter or longer

For a shorter reading, keep the opening, the main decision and the final paragraph. To expand it, let your child add one detail about the setting. Choose the stopping point before adding another adventure, so the story has an ending tonight.

Reading with siblings

Read the main action simply for a younger listener, then offer an optional question to an older child. Our two-year-old stories offer shorter alternatives. Compare the 4-year-old guide or return to the age hub for the full choice.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I read to my 5-year-old at bedtime?

Choose a story or an agreed stopping point that fits your evening. Shorten it when your child is tired, and leave a longer story for another time. There is no reading-time target on this page.

Should my five-year-old read the story independently?

No. These are read-aloud stories for a parent or caregiver. Your child can join in if they want to, but bedtime does not need to become reading practice.

Sources behind the reading guidance

These stories and practical suggestions are website originals. Age labels are starting points, not developmental tests. ZERO TO THREE describes repetition, familiar subjects and following a toddler’s interest in Read Early and Often. The American Academy of Pediatrics encourages children to choose books, including favorites they want repeated, in Tips to Help Your Child Enjoy Reading Aloud.
