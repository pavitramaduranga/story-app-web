Bedtime Stories for 5-Year-Olds: 3 Free Stories

Choose one of three complete stories about making a map, delivering a letter or finishing a paper town.

Choose one story for tonight

  • The Moonlight Letter — Zoe follows a familiar clue to the right door.
  • The Bridge in the Paper Town — Eli finds a new way to join two streets.

1. The Map for Tomorrow

Leo spread a piece of paper on the kitchen table.

“I am making a map for tomorrow,” he told Auntie.

He drew the back door, the apple tree and the little patch of mint. Then he drew a large blue circle.

“Is that a pond?” Auntie asked.

“A pond we could build.”

Auntie sat beside him. “Let us put things that are already there on this map. We can make another page for ideas.”

Leo drew a small flowerpot over the blue circle. There was a blue pot by the apple tree, so that fitted nicely.

He added a dotted path from the door to the pot. But the path went straight through the mint.

Leo remembered how carefully they had planted it. He rubbed out a few dots and curved the path around the edge instead.

“Now nobody steps on the leaves,” he said.

At the bottom of the page, Leo drew a square. He could not remember what stood beside the gate.

“We can look in the morning,” said Auntie.

Leo wanted the map to be finished. He held his pencil above the square.

Then he drew a tiny question mark inside it.

“That means we have something to notice tomorrow.”

Auntie found a magnet and helped him put the map on the fridge. Beside it, they pinned a blank page for garden ideas.

Leo washed his hands and chose a book for bedtime.

In bed, he thought of the dotted path curling around the mint. He knew where it began. He knew where it would take him.

And the small square by the gate could wait.

“My map has a resting place,” he said.

“Where?” asked Auntie.

“Here. Until morning.”

2. The Moonlight Letter

Zoe and Dad were closing the front gate when Zoe spotted a white envelope under the hedge.

On the front was a drawing of a yellow door, a round window and three flowerpots. There was no name.

“I know that door,” Zoe said. “It belongs to someone on our street.”

They walked past the blue door with the square window. They passed the red door with one tall pot. Neither matched.

Near the corner stood a yellow door with a round window. Two flowerpots sat on the step.

“Only two,” Dad said.

Zoe looked carefully. A third pot rested beside the watering can, where the moonlight made its leaves shine.

She rang the bell. Mr. Sen opened the door in his slippers.

“My sister drew that envelope so I would know it was from her,” he said. “It must have slipped from my bag.”

He thanked Zoe and placed the letter safely on his hall table.

On the way home, Zoe counted the doors they passed: yellow, red, blue, then their own green door beneath the porch light.

Dad closed the gate. Zoe closed the front door. The letter was home, and so was she.

3. The Bridge in the Paper Town

Eli built a paper town across the bedroom rug. A cereal-box library stood near the pillow hill, and four folded houses lined the blue-ribbon river.

There was one problem. The cardboard bridge was too short. It reached from one bank and stopped above the water.

Eli tried a longer strip of paper, but it bent in the middle. He tried moving the river, but then it ran under the library.

His cousin Mara picked up two small boxes that had been waiting to become shops.

“What if the bridge does not cross in one jump?” she asked.

They placed one box in the middle of the river as an island. The cardboard reached the island, but not the far bank.

Eli turned the second box on its side. Now the two boxes made a wide, steady middle, and the cardboard joined both streets.

A paper bus crossed first. Then a tiny paper cat. Nothing bent or fell.

Eli parked the bus beside the library. Mara placed the cat inside the last house.

They left the bridge in place and put the unused paper in a neat pile for tomorrow.

When Dad switched off the lamp, the ribbon river disappeared into the dark. The bridge stayed exactly where it belonged, holding the quiet town together.

Choosing a story for your 5-year-old

Choose the premise that sounds most interesting tonight. If your child enjoys predicting, pause once before a character decides what to do. Questions are optional, and there is no need to turn the story into reading practice.

Use your child’s interests and response as your guide. Age categories help you browse; they do not mean a child must prefer a certain story length. A familiar picture book is still an option.

Make the story shorter or longer

For a shorter reading, keep the opening, the main decision and the final paragraph. To expand it, let your child add one detail about the setting. Choose the stopping point before adding another adventure, so the story has an ending tonight.

Reading with siblings

Read the main action simply for a younger listener, then offer an optional question to an older child. Our two-year-old stories offer shorter alternatives. Compare the 4-year-old guide or return to the age hub for the full choice.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I read to my 5-year-old at bedtime?

Choose a story or an agreed stopping point that fits your evening. Shorten it when your child is tired, and leave a longer story for another time. There is no reading-time target on this page.

Should my five-year-old read the story independently?

No. These are read-aloud stories for a parent or caregiver. Your child can join in if they want to, but bedtime does not need to become reading practice.
