Bedtime stories for 2-year-olds

Try a tiny story about something your child knows: a cup, boots or a toy bus. Use a repeating phrase if they enjoy joining in. Our three free bedtime stories for two-year-olds include The Little Cup, Duck’s Two Boots and The Little Bus Is Home, plus ways to shorten each reading.

A short sample: The Little Cup

A little blue cup stood on the kitchen table.

“Who will sit beside me?” wondered the cup.

A small plate came first. “I will,” said the plate.

A soft napkin came next. “I will too.”

Then a spoon arrived with a tiny clink.

“Not too loud,” said the cup. “We are having a quiet tea.”

A child poured a little water into the cup. The plate held a slice of pear. The napkin waited for a drip.

They sat together while the child ate the pear and took a sip.

When tea was finished, the cup had a wash. The plate had a wash. The spoon had a wash too.

The napkin folded into a small square.

“Will you sit beside me tomorrow?” asked the cup.

“Yes,” said the plate, the spoon and the napkin.

And the little blue cup rested on its shelf.

Read Duck’s Two Boots next, or stop here if one story is enough.

Bedtime stories for 3-year-olds

Try a familiar setting with one small problem: a blanket to find or a train arriving home. A child who enjoys talking might help choose the ending. The three-year-old guide includes seven adaptable outlines and optional parent prompts. These are story starters, so choose this guide when you want to tell a story in your own words.

Bedtime stories for 4-year-olds

Try an everyday decision with a clear outcome. In The Bench Beside the Gate, Mina works out how to share her reading place with Grandpa. Read the complete four-year-old bedtime story for a gentle problem-solving theme and three more outlines.

Bedtime stories for 5-year-olds

Try a small plan with a beginning, a change and a settled ending. In The Map for Tomorrow, Leo makes a garden map and leaves one question for morning. The five-year-old story guide offers the complete story and ways to adapt it without turning bedtime into a reading lesson.

What if your child prefers another age’s stories?

Use whichever guide suits them. An older child may want a tiny familiar story; a younger child may enjoy listening to a longer one. You can skip descriptions, repeat favorite lines or finish early. For siblings, choose one story everyone likes and make questions optional.

Build a small reading routine

Let your child choose between two stories, read together, then use the same closing phrase if that suits your family. Sources behind the reading guidance

These stories and practical suggestions are website originals. Age labels are starting points, not developmental tests. ZERO TO THREE describes repetition, familiar subjects and following a toddler’s interest in Read Early and Often. The American Academy of Pediatrics encourages children to choose books, including favorites they want repeated, in Tips to Help Your Child Enjoy Reading Aloud.
