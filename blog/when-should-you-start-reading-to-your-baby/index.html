When Should You Start Reading to Your Baby? Do They Even Understand?

Start from birth, or even during late pregnancy if you enjoy it. Your baby will not follow the story at first, but they are already learning your voice, rhythm, closeness, and the first patterns of language.

Written by the Baboo Stories team · 12 minute read

Many parents feel a little silly reading to a newborn. You sit there with a tiny baby who cannot hold their head up, cannot follow a story, cannot answer questions, and may spend most of story time staring at your face, drifting off, or trying to mouth the corner of a book.

So it is natural to wonder: when should I actually start reading to my child? And just as importantly, do babies even understand what I am saying?

The short answer is simple: you can start from birth. Your baby will not understand the plot the way an older child does. They will not know why the bear is looking for his hat or why the moon says goodnight. But they are still learning. From the earliest days, babies absorb the sound of your voice, the rhythm of language, your facial expressions, your tone, and the comfort of being close to you.

Quick Answer

Start reading to your baby from birth. Before babies understand words, they still learn from your voice, rhythm, repetition, closeness, facial expressions, and the calm routine of shared attention.

Can you start reading before birth?

Yes, you can start reading before birth if it feels natural to you. Babies begin hearing sounds before they are born, and by the later stages of pregnancy, they may be able to hear familiar voices from outside the womb.

That does not mean a baby in the womb understands a story, learns vocabulary, or follows a plot. But reading aloud during pregnancy can still be meaningful because your baby may become familiar with the rhythm of your voice.

Think of pregnancy reading as bonding, not teaching. If you enjoy reading, singing, or talking to your baby before birth, it can become a calming ritual for you too. A familiar book, lullaby, or short story may even feel comforting after birth because your voice is already one of the most familiar sounds in your baby's world. For a deeper look at this stage, read our guide on reading to baby in the womb.

Do babies understand when you read to them?

Babies do not understand books in the way adults do. A newborn does not understand plot. A three-month-old does not know that a character is lost. A six-month-old is not following every sentence from beginning to end.

But babies understand more than we sometimes realize. They begin by understanding your voice, your rhythm, your tone, your facial expressions, the feeling of being held, and the repetition of familiar sounds.

Before babies understand words, they understand patterns. They notice when your voice rises and falls. They hear pauses. They sense excitement, softness, surprise, and comfort. Over time, these small moments help build the foundation for language.

So the goal is not for your baby to understand every word. The goal is for your baby to experience language as something warm, familiar, and shared.

Why reading matters before a child can talk

It may seem strange that reading helps before a baby can speak. But language development starts long before the first word.

Babies are listening from the beginning. Every time you talk, sing, narrate, or read, your child hears the sounds and patterns of language. Books often contain words and sentence structures that are different from everyday speech, which gives babies richer language exposure.

Daily conversation may include simple phrases like "Here is your bottle," "Time for a diaper change," or "Let us go to sleep." Books introduce a different kind of language: "The moon glowed softly above the quiet trees," or "The little train puffed over the hill." Your baby may not understand those sentences yet, but their brain is hearing how language works.

At this stage, story time is less about the book and more about the experience. Your baby is learning that your voice means comfort, that books belong in calm moments, and that language is something shared. Over time, this becomes part of the larger benefits of story time, from bonding to early language development.

Reading is also about bonding

One of the biggest benefits of reading to babies has nothing to do with letters. Reading creates closeness.

When you read to your baby, they are often in your lap, near your chest, hearing your familiar voice. They feel your warmth. They see your face. They experience your attention. This helps reading become associated with safety, affection, and calm.

That emotional connection matters. A baby who associates books with comfort is more likely to enjoy books later. Reading becomes not just an academic activity, but a relationship activity.

Reading by age: what babies learn at each stage

Newborn to 3 months

At this stage, your baby's vision is still developing. They may not focus clearly on book illustrations, especially small or detailed ones. But they can hear your voice.

For newborns, you can read high-contrast baby books, simple board books, nursery rhymes, poems, a favorite children's story, or even your own reading material in a warm voice. The content is not the main thing yet. The sound of your voice is.

Keep reading short and gentle. A few minutes is enough. If your baby looks away, cries, yawns, or gets fussy, stop and try again later.

3 to 6 months

Around this age, babies may become more alert during reading. They may watch your face, smile, kick, coo, or respond to the sound of your voice.

Good books for this stage include books with faces, bright colors, simple pictures, rhymes, repetitive sounds, and soft cloth pages. This is a good time to exaggerate your expressions. Smile, widen your eyes, use different voices, and pause between sounds. Your baby is learning that communication goes back and forth.

6 to 12 months

This is the age when reading can become more physical. Your baby may grab the book, slap the pages, chew the corners, drop it, close it, or try to turn several pages at once. This is normal. In fact, it is a sign of interest.

Choose sturdy board books, bath books, touch-and-feel books, books with simple photos, and books showing babies, animals, food, or familiar objects. You do not have to read every word. You can point and name things: "Dog." "Baby." "Ball." "Where is the cat?" This kind of interaction is extremely valuable.

12 to 18 months

Toddlers often become more active participants. They may bring books to you, point at pictures, turn pages, make animal sounds, or request the same book again and again.

Repetition can feel tiring for adults, but it is powerful for children. Hearing the same words repeatedly helps toddlers predict what comes next. Prediction builds memory, confidence, and language.

18 to 24 months

At this stage, many children begin to understand simple story sequences. They may remember favorite lines, finish repeated phrases, or pretend to read aloud.

Once your baby becomes a toddler, reading becomes much more interactive. They may point, interrupt, repeat favorite words, skip pages, or ask for the same book again and again. This is a good stage to shift from simply reading aloud to inviting participation. For practical ideas, see our guide on how to read aloud to toddlers.

Ages 2 to 4

By this age, children often begin to understand characters, emotions, cause and effect, and simple plots. They may ask questions, correct you if you skip a page, or "read" the story from memory.

This is a great time to build comprehension by talking about the story. Ask simple questions like "Why is she sad?", "What would you do?", "Where did the truck go?", or "Can you find the red shoes?" These questions help children connect books with thinking, not just listening.

What if my baby does not sit still?

A baby or toddler who crawls away during story time is not failing at reading. A toddler who flips pages too quickly is not doing it wrong. A child who wants the same book five times in a row is not being difficult. These are normal early reading behaviors.

Young children learn through movement, repetition, and sensory exploration. Read for shorter periods, let them stand or move nearby, choose interactive books, read during meals or bath time, and follow their attention instead of forcing the page order.

If story time feels difficult later in toddlerhood, the answer is usually not to force longer reading sessions. It is to make reading feel playful, short, and emotionally rewarding. Our guide on how to make toddlers love story time has more ideas for children who resist books or lose interest quickly.

How long should you read each day?

You do not need a long, perfect reading session. For babies, even 5 minutes a day can be meaningful. As your child grows, you can slowly increase the time. Some days you may read several books. Other days you may only read one page. That is fine.

Consistency matters more than length. Many families find that reading works best as part of a predictable evening rhythm. A short book before sleep can signal that the day is slowing down. If nights feel chaotic, you may want to build reading into a simple bedtime routine rather than treating it as a separate task.

What should you read to a newborn?

For a newborn, almost anything read aloud can be useful. You do not need to worry too much about the story. High-contrast baby books, simple rhyming books, lullabies, short poems, classic picture books, and books with repetitive language all work well.

As your child grows, the best story changes with their age and attention span. A baby may enjoy rhythm and faces, while a three-year-old may want characters, choices, and simple problems to solve. For more age-specific ideas, see our guide to bedtime stories by age.

Can you read in any language?

Yes. Read in the language you are most comfortable speaking. Some parents worry that reading in more than one language will confuse a baby. In most cases, multilingual exposure is a strength. Babies are capable of hearing differences between languages, and hearing rich language from loving caregivers is beneficial.

The most important thing is not which language you choose. It is that your baby hears warm, responsive, meaningful speech.

Do audiobooks or screens count?

Audiobooks can be useful, especially for older children, but for babies and toddlers they do not fully replace reading with a caregiver. The biggest benefits of early reading come from interaction: eye contact, touch, facial expression, pausing, responding to sounds, pointing at pictures, and naming what your child notices.

If your goal is a calmer evening, physical books and parent-led stories are usually better than fast-moving videos before bed. Screen-based stories can be useful in moderation, but they should not replace the back-and-forth connection that makes early reading powerful. You can read more about this in our article on toddler screen time before bed.

Common parent worries

My baby just chews the book. Is that okay?

Yes. Babies explore with their mouths. Use sturdy board books, cloth books, or bath books, and think of chewing as early book exploration.

My toddler keeps skipping pages. Should I stop them?

Not usually. Letting your child control the pace sometimes helps story time feel playful and shared. You can always return to the missed pages another day.

We missed the newborn stage. Is it too late?

No. Start now. Children benefit from being read to at every age, and a warm routine can begin whenever your family is ready.

So, when is the best time to start?

The best time to start reading to your child is from birth. But the deeper answer is this: start when you can, keep it gentle, and make it a routine your child associates with warmth.

Your baby may not understand the story at first. But they understand your voice. They understand closeness. They understand rhythm, repetition, comfort, and attention. Those are the earliest building blocks of language and literacy.

Reading to a baby is not wasted effort. It is one of the simplest ways to say: you are safe, I am here, and language is part of love.
