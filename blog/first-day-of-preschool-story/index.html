First Day of Preschool Story: Help With Goodbye Worries

Read “Mina and the Pocket-Sized Hello,” then use a simple first-day sequence, three gentle questions and a practical plan for goodbye worries.

Written by the Baboo Stories team · About 9 min read

A first day can feel exciting and uncomfortable at the same time. This original story gives children a simple sequence they can picture: a short goodbye, a safe grown-up, one small classroom success, and a caregiver who comes back.

First day of preschool story: Mina and the Pocket-Sized Hello

On Mina's first preschool morning, her yellow backpack looked much bigger than usual.

Mum put Mina's water bottle inside. Mina added a small box of apple slices. Then she tucked in a folded paper heart that they had coloured together.

“The heart can stay in your pocket,” Mum said. “When you touch it, you can remember our goodbye and our hello after preschool.”

Mina held the paper heart tightly. Her tummy felt wobbly.

“What if I miss you?” she asked.

“You probably will,” Mum said gently. “Missing someone means they matter to you. Miss Elena will help you while I am away, and I will come back after fruit time and garden time.”

Mina knew those words. They had practised them at home:

“A hug, a wave, then Mum comes back.”

They said the words together while they put on their shoes.

At preschool, the blue gate was open. Children carried bags, drawings and toy animals. Somewhere inside, wooden blocks clicked together. A bell made a soft ting.

Miss Elena waited beside a row of little hooks. She wore a green scarf with tiny suns on it.

“Good morning, Mina,” she said. “Your hook has a ladybird picture. Would you like me to show you?”

Mina stood close to Mum. She did not feel ready to look at a hook.

Mum knelt beside her. “It is time for our goodbye.”

Mina's eyes filled with tears. “I don't want the goodbye.”

“Goodbyes can feel hard,” Mum said. “We can do our three things.”

Mina pressed the paper heart in her pocket.

First came one warm hug.

Then Mina and Mum said, “A hug, a wave, then Mum comes back.”

Last came one slow wave.

Mum walked through the gate. She did not disappear all at once. She turned at the corner, waved again, and then went on her way.

Mina cried for a little while. Miss Elena stayed close.

“You wanted Mum to stay,” she said. “I will sit with you.”

Miss Elena did not hurry Mina. She pointed to a small window beside the door.

“We can watch the red bus pass, or we can find your ladybird hook.”

Mina took two shaky breaths. “The ladybird.”

Together they found it. Mina slipped off one backpack strap, then the other. The bag hung on the hook with a small bump.

“You did the first job,” said Miss Elena.

The classroom had a paint table, a reading rug and a shelf of wooden animals. At one table, a child called Arlo was making a road from flat blocks. His road stopped at a cardboard river.

Mina watched from beside Miss Elena.

“The cars can't cross,” Arlo said.

Mina noticed two long blocks on the shelf. She picked up one and held it over the river. It reached only halfway.

Arlo brought the other block. They placed the ends together.

“A bridge,” Mina whispered.

Arlo rolled a green car across. It bumped in the middle but reached the other side.

“It works!” he said.

Mina rolled a yellow car next. Then they made a parking place from four small blocks.

For a few minutes, Mina forgot to hold her paper heart.

When the bell made its soft ting, the children sat for fruit time. Mina opened her box, but one apple slice slipped onto the table.

Arlo passed her a little cloth.

“Here,” he said.

Mina wiped the table. She ate two apple slices and saved the last one for the end.

She remembered Mum's words: after fruit time and garden time.

“Is Mum coming now?” Mina asked.

“Not yet,” Miss Elena said. “First we have garden time. Then we will wash our hands and listen for families at the gate.”

Mina touched the heart in her pocket. There were still steps to go, but now she knew where she was in the day.

In the garden, Mina saw chalk, buckets and a low wooden boat. She and Arlo drew a winding blue river on the ground. They added a yellow bridge and three round stepping stones.

A younger child stood nearby, holding a piece of pink chalk.

“Would you like to draw a fish?” Mina asked.

The child nodded and made a fish shaped like a potato. Mina gave it three purple bubbles.

Soon Miss Elena clapped a quiet rhythm. The children put the chalk into its tub and carried the buckets to the wall.

Mina washed the blue dust from her fingers. Her tummy gave another wobble, but this wobble felt different. It was an almost-time wobble.

At the window, a child called, “My grandpa!”

Another called, “My dad!”

Mina looked through the glass. For one moment, she saw coats, bags and moving feet. Then she saw Mum's purple umbrella, even though it had not rained. Mum had brought it because Mina always spotted it quickly.

Mina ran to the gate.

Mum opened her arms. “Hello after preschool.”

Mina hugged her. “You came back after fruit time and garden time.”

“I did.”

Mina pulled the paper heart from her pocket. One corner had become soft from all her pressing.

“I cried,” she said. “Miss Elena sat with me. Then I made a bridge. It had a bump, but the car got across.”

Mum smiled. “You had a hard feeling, and you found your way through the morning.”

On the walk home, Mina held Mum's hand. The preschool gate no longer looked quite so big.

“Tomorrow,” Mina said, “can the heart go in my pocket again?”

“Of course.”

Mina thought about the ladybird hook, the cardboard river and the potato-shaped fish.

Tomorrow might still have a wobbly goodbye. But Mina knew the shape of the day now: a hug, a wave, a safe grown-up, something small to do, and Mum coming back.

A parent note for first-day worries

A story can help a child picture what may happen, but it cannot guarantee a tear-free goodbye. Keep your real plan brief and truthful. Name the adult who will help, say when you will return in a way your child can understand, and follow the setting’s arrival guidance.

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends preparing children for a new school routine and keeping goodbyes brief. PBS KIDS also suggests acknowledging separation feelings and helping children know what to expect. Every child and preschool is different.

Three gentle questions

  • Which part of Mina's first morning felt hardest for her?
  • What small job helped Mina begin to feel comfortable?
  • What could you keep in your pocket to remember that your grown-up will return?

A simple first-day story plan

Goodbye ritual → classroom moment → caregiver returns

  1. Choose one goodbye. Try one squeeze, one short phrase, and one wave.
  2. Name one safe adult. Use the teacher’s real name if you know it.
  3. Picture one manageable moment. Hanging a bag, finding a toy, or watching an activity is enough.
  4. Describe the return. Use an event your child can picture, such as “after outside time,” if that matches the real schedule.

Practise the sequence with toys. Let your child play the caregiver, teacher, child, or observer. If your child changes the story, follow their lead and keep the ending honest.

Questions parents often ask

Can a story stop first-day preschool tears?

A story can make the sequence more familiar, but it cannot guarantee a tear-free goodbye. Use it alongside truthful preparation and the preschool’s own transition plan.

What should I say when I will come back?

Use a real event your child can picture, such as after lunch or outside time, and make sure it matches the actual schedule.

Should I sneak away if my child is distracted?

A brief, predictable goodbye usually gives a child clearer information than disappearing without saying goodbye. Follow the preschool’s guidance for your child and setting.
